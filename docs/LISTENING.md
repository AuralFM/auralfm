# The listening problem

Notes on Roshanak Kheshti, *"Touching Listening: The Aural Imaginary in the World
Music Culture Industry,"* American Quarterly 63:3 (2011), 711–731 — and what it
does to AuralFM's brand story.

Read it as a warning before reading it as a resource. **This paper is a critique
of an industry AuralFM structurally resembles**, and the brand is stronger for
answering it than for not having noticed.

---

## What the paper argues

Kheshti spent 2002–4 doing ethnography inside a San Francisco world music label.
Her argument is that world music sells a particular *listening position*: the
pleasure is not in the music alone but in a fantasy of contact with a racialised,
gendered elsewhere, constructed by the listener and carefully staged by the
industry. She calls the space where that happens the **aural imaginary**.

Three moves matter for us.

**Listening is bodily, not just aural.** She opens with the deaf percussionist
Evelyn Glennie — *"I think I hear it through here, but I also hear it through my
hands, my arms, my cheekbones, my scalp"* — and Don Ihde: *"The ears may be focal
'organs' of hearing, but one listens with his whole body"* (713–14). Hearing is a
form of touch.

**The senses were split up for profit.** Following Benjamin, she traces how
industrial capitalism produced *"representational value"* — the commodity
consumed through an isolated eye — and how an *"acoustic modernity"* paired with
it (714). Sensory specialisation is not natural; it is infrastructure.

**And the industry trains the ear.** The label *"curates and guides the listening
event, training the consumer-listener how to function as what I call an 'ear
agent'"* (717).

## The passages that are about us, whether we like it or not

### 1. The credo

> Kinship's credo **"everything is closer than you think"** de-emphasizes cultural
> differences, be they linguistic, religious, or aesthetic, emphasizing instead
> the universality of music and multicultural aesthetic pleasure. (718)

That is the single most uncomfortable line in the paper, because it is four words
away from ours. *A window onto the world you can listen to.* **Everything is
closer than you think.** Same promise, same mechanism: distance collapsed into
availability.

Kheshti's objection is not that the claim is false. It is that collapsing
distance is exactly what makes the pleasure consumable — difference flattened
into "the universality of music," which costs the listener nothing.

### 2. Discovery as the thing itself

> world music creates a voyage of discovery, a sonic experience of contact, an
> auditory deflowering that penetrates the harmony of difference.
> — Steven Feld, quoted at 722

A photoreal globe you spin until you find somewhere is a **voyage-of-discovery
machine**. That is the product, said plainly. The README's own demo beat — *spin
from Houston to Rio, hear it* — is Feld's sentence with a UI.

### 3. The position the product builds, regardless of who is using it

> the culture of world music's circulation constitutes and constructs a white,
> male listening subject, **regardless of the actual listeners' bodies**. (721)

This one needs saying directly. The maintainer is a Black designer, and that does
not exempt the product. Kheshti's claim is about the position a product
*constructs*, not about who occupies it. A globe that offers the world's sound as
available-on-click builds that position by default, and only stops building it by
design.

### 4. The pleasure of not understanding

> rarely does the record label provide lyrical translations, foregrounding
> instead the sonic qualities of the vocals … Overall, it is the sound that is
> emphasized as meaningful in world music (715–16)

Her listeners say the songs *"need no translation."* The other stays sonic,
affective, and unintelligible — and the unintelligibility is the product.

### 5. Why "culture vulture" is the wrong critique

> Debates centered on world music as exploitative have missed the point by
> focusing exclusively on labor-relations or have taken the form of the
> "culture-vulture" critique, viewing culture primarily as intellectual property,
> with critique narrowly focused on royalties, authorship, and compensation. What
> has scarcely been addressed is the geopolitical, historical, libidinal,
> discursive, and affective formations that structure desire and pleasure. (722)

Worth pinning up. The licensing is clean, every source is public domain or
permissive, nobody is unpaid — **and that settles none of what she is asking.**
The question is what shape of desire the product manufactures.

## Where AuralFM already answers her — three structural differences

These are not spin. They are architectural, and they are the brand story.

### There is no producer

Kheshti's entire apparatus is mediation: liner notes, album artwork, "modern
production values," the label president telling a radio host what to listen for
in Khoi San music — *"songs about the land, about nature, about family, and I
would assume that's probably what we're hearing here"* (717). The listener is
taught what they are hearing by someone selling it.

**AuralFM has nobody in that chair.** The station is broadcasting to its own
local audience, on its own schedule, for its own reasons. You are not being
served a curated elsewhere; you are overhearing something not addressed to you.
That is a categorically different relation, and it is the most defensible thing
about the product.

The corollary is a constraint: **every editorial layer we add moves us toward
Kinship.** Tags, "top stations," recommended places, curated collections,
mood-based browsing — each one puts someone back in the producer's chair. That is
now a design rule with a reason behind it, not a preference.

### Captions are the inversion, not a feature

This is the big one, and it reframes #30 entirely.

Kheshti's industry withholds translation because the pleasure depends on not
understanding. **Captions do the opposite: they insist on comprehension.** The
moment a Tehran broadcast becomes a sentence you can read, it stops being
texture and becomes speech — someone talking about traffic, or a subsidy, or the
weather, in a place you were about to find atmospheric.

That is the single most anti-aural-imaginary thing the product could do, and it
is currently parked as a performance problem. **The performance problem is real
and the priority argument just changed.** Captions are not a nice-to-have; on
this reading they are the ethical core of the app.

### They can answer back

The aural imaginary is one-directional by construction. The listener builds the
other; the other never speaks. Geohash chat breaks that — someone in the cell
you are listening to can reply, and you are no longer the only one authoring the
encounter.

`docs/DECK.md` already flags that "a window is one-way" undersells this. Kheshti
supplies the reason it matters: one-way *is* the mechanism she is criticising.

## What this should change

1. **The tagline is now a known problem with a known cause.** "A window onto the
   world you can listen to" is beautiful and it is Kinship's credo in nicer
   clothes — one-way, distance-collapsing, the world rendered available. The deck
   already suspected this. This is the argument for fixing it.
2. **Promote captions (#30).** Reframed from feature to thesis.
3. **A copy rule:** never describe a place as a sound. *"Hear what Lagos sounds
   like"* is the aural imaginary in five words. *"Hear what Lagos is listening
   to"* is not — it points at people with their own attention, not at ambience.
   The README already gets this right in places and wrong in others.
4. **Hold the line on curation.** Every editorial layer is a step toward the
   thing being critiqued.
5. **The globe needs its own answer.** Kheshti via Benjamin is hard on
   ocularcentrism and "representational value" (714), and we sell listening with
   a photoreal picture. The available defence is a real one: the globe does not
   depict *people*. It depicts position, distance, daylight, cloud, and whether
   the band is open — the physical conditions of contact, not a representation of
   culture. That is worth saying out loud in the brand language rather than
   assuming it reads.

## How to keep doing this

The method that made this useful: **find the field that has already spent thirty
years criticising what you are building, and read its critics before its
enthusiasts.** For a listening product that field is sound studies, and it is
unusually well developed.

Straight from Kheshti's own citations, which is the most reliable way to build a
reading list:

| | Why |
|---|---|
| **Kheshti, *Modernity's Ear*** (NYU Press, 2015) | The book this article became. It adds **radio** to the analysis alongside film and recording, and traces the early "songcatchers." The obvious next read. |
| **Jonathan Sterne, *The Audible Past*** (2003) | Where "audile technique" and the individuation of the listener come from. The prehistory of every pair of headphones. |
| **Steven Feld, "A Sweet Lullaby for World Music"** | The origin story of the genre and its colonial inheritance. Source of the "voyage of discovery" line. |
| **Radano & Bohlman, *Music and the Racial Imagination*** | The "racial imagination" — the concept Kheshti builds on and argues past. |
| **Don Ihde, *Listening and Voice*** | The phenomenology. "One listens with his whole body." |
| **Emily Thompson, *The Soundscape of Modernity*** | Acoustic space as designed infrastructure. |
| **David Suisman, *Selling Sounds*** | How music became a commodity in the first place. |
| **Eve Sedgwick, *Touching, Feeling*** | The texture-and-affect frame behind Kheshti's title. |

Two directions she does not cover that this product specifically needs:

- **Radio studies proper** — Susan Douglas, *Listening In*; Michele Hilmes,
  *Radio Voices*. Kheshti is about recordings. AuralFM is about live transmission,
  which has a different politics: it is civic, local, and mostly not addressed to
  outsiders. **That difference is an asset and nobody has written our version of
  it.**
- **Acoustic ecology** — Schafer's *The Soundscape*, and the field-recording
  world. Directly relevant to the sound-maps idea in roadmap §3, and it carries
  its own well-rehearsed argument about recording places you do not live in.

### Using it without sounding like it

The research does not belong in the marketing copy. Nobody buys a radio app
because it read Kheshti. What it should do is make the copy *unbluffable*: every
claim survives someone who has read the literature, and the product's real
distinction — no producer, translation on, they can reply — gets described in
plain words that happen to be defensible all the way down.

The strongest version of the story is not *"a beautiful way to hear the world."*
It is: **the world is already talking, to itself, and you can listen in — and if
you want, understand, and answer.** That is a different product from the one
Kheshti spent two years inside, and the difference is worth being able to defend.
