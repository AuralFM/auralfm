# More for #library

Melton's structure, which is cleaner than the original: **#library is a reference
directory about radio itself** — licensing, history, how it works. Nothing about
AuralFM goes in it. The project's own material moves to a second forum.

Three new posts below. With the four already published, #library becomes a
genuine learning database rather than a project blog.

**The rule that applies to all of it:** never encourage transmitting without a
licence. Listening is unregulated almost everywhere; operating a transmitter is
not. The history posts are history, and the licensing post is the answer to
anyone who wants to do more than listen.

## Post A — Getting licensed, and why you probably do not need to

*Category: Licensing. The most practically useful thing in the library, and the
one that keeps the pirate history honest.*

> **Start here, because it saves most people the trouble: you do not need a
> licence to listen.** Not to shortwave, not to amateur bands, not to anything.
> Receiving has never been licensed in the United States, the UK, or most of the
> world. Every receiver in AuralFM is somebody listening, legally, to signals
> that arrive at their antenna uninvited.
>
> A licence is for **transmitting**. If you only ever want to hear things, stop
> reading — you are already allowed.
>
> **If you do want to transmit**, the amateur radio service exists precisely for
> that, and it is far easier to get into than most people assume.
>
> **In the United States** there are three classes, and each is a multiple-choice
> exam with no Morse code requirement — that was dropped in 2007.
>
> | Class | Exam | What it opens |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Technician** | 35 questions, 26 to pass | All VHF/UHF, plus limited HF — 10 m voice, and CW on 80, 40 and 15 m |
> | **General** | 35 questions, 26 to pass | Most of HF. This is the one that gets you talking across oceans. |
> | **Amateur Extra** | 50 questions, 37 to pass | Everything, including the exclusive sub-bands |
>
> **The question pool is public.** Every question on every exam is published in
> advance, word for word. That is not a loophole — it is deliberate, because the
> service would rather you learn the material than be tested on surprise. Free
> practice sites drill the exact pool.
>
> Exams are administered by volunteers, usually for around $15, often at a club
> on a Saturday morning. The FCC then charges a $35 application fee for the
> licence itself, which lasts ten years. You get a callsign, and it is yours.
>
> **In the UK** it is Ofcom, and three levels: Foundation, Intermediate, Full.
> Foundation is deliberately gentle and can be done in a weekend course.
>
> **Elsewhere**, most countries follow the same general shape under the
> international allocations, and reciprocal arrangements mean a licence often
> travels.
>
> **What it costs you beyond the exam:** a callsign is a public record. Amateur
> licences are searchable, and in the US that has historically included an
> address. Worth knowing before you apply, particularly if you have reason to
> care.
>
> **Why anyone bothers.** Because receiving is a one-way relationship and some
> people want the other half — to answer, to be answered, to build something and
> find out whether it works by whether somebody a thousand miles away hears it.
> The whole culture around emergency communication, the mesh networks, the
> propagation beacons and most of the receivers in this app come from people who
> went and got licensed.

## Post B — A short history of radio

*Category: History. The one that gives everything else a spine.*

> **1887.** Heinrich Hertz proves electromagnetic waves exist by generating them
> in a laboratory and detecting them across the room. Asked what use it might
> have, he reportedly said none whatsoever.
>
> **1895–1901.** Guglielmo Marconi turns the demonstration into a business,
> pushing the distance out year by year, and in December 1901 claims reception of
> a signal sent from Cornwall to Newfoundland. It was three faint dots — the
> letter S — and people argued about whether he really heard it for decades.
> Nobody argued about what happened next.
>
> **1906.** Reginald Fessenden is said to have transmitted voice and music on
> Christmas Eve to ships off the Atlantic coast. The evidence is thin and
> historians still fight about it. What is certain is that within a few years
> voice transmission worked and the dots-and-dashes era had a rival.
>
> **1912.** The *Titanic* sinks, and radio's role in the rescue — and the chaos
> of amateurs clogging the frequencies afterwards — produces the Radio Act. Radio
> becomes something governments regulate, and amateurs are pushed to "the useless
> short waves above 1.5 MHz." That exile turned out to be the discovery of the
> century: those useless frequencies bounce off the ionosphere.
>
> **1920.** KDKA in Pittsburgh broadcasts election returns and is usually called
> the first licensed commercial station. Within four years there are hundreds. A
> technology for point-to-point messages becomes a technology for talking to
> everyone at once, which nobody had planned.
>
> **The 1930s.** International shortwave broadcasting begins in earnest. The BBC
> Empire Service opens in 1932, aimed at a scattered empire. Others follow
> quickly, and the reason is never only cultural.
>
> **The war and after.** Radio becomes an instrument of state. Voice of America
> starts in 1942. Radio Free Europe and Radio Liberty follow at the end of the
> decade, aimed across the Iron Curtain. Radio Moscow aims back. Jamming becomes
> an industry — transmitters built for no purpose but to make other transmitters
> unintelligible.
>
> **The long decline.** Satellite, then cable, then the internet take the
> audience. Broadcasters shut transmitters through the 1990s and 2000s. The BBC
> ends most of its shortwave services. Sites that ran megawatts go quiet.
>
> **And it did not die.** What remains is smaller, stranger and in some ways more
> interesting: broadcasters aiming at people with no other way to receive them,
> religious stations that bought the abandoned transmitters, utility traffic that
> never needed an audience, numbers stations still reading digits into the dark,
> and hobbyists who put receivers on the internet so anyone can borrow an
> antenna.
>
> The technology outlived its own golden age and became infrastructure again.

## Post C — A short history of shortwave

*Category: History. The sequel to Post B, and where the politics lives.*

> Shortwave's whole history is a consequence of one accident: the "useless"
> frequencies amateurs were exiled to in 1912 turned out to reach further than
> anything else on Earth.
>
> **The 1920s.** Amateurs prove it. Transatlantic contacts on short waves, made
> with home-built equipment and a fraction of the power the commercial stations
> used. Within a few years everybody wants those frequencies back.
>
> **The 1930s — empire, then propaganda.** The BBC Empire Service opens in 1932.
> Germany, Italy and the Soviet Union build international services through the
> decade, and the purpose is explicit: reach people inside somebody else's
> borders and speak to them directly, past their own government. That capability
> defines everything that follows.
>
> **The Cold War.** Shortwave becomes the primary contested medium. VOA, the BBC
> World Service, Radio Free Europe, Radio Liberty, Deutsche Welle, Radio Moscow,
> Radio Peking, Radio Havana. Enormous transmitter sites, some of them megawatts.
>
> And **jamming** at the same scale — the Soviet Union ran a jamming network
> whose power consumption rivalled the broadcasting it was blocking. Two states
> spending real money to make each other unintelligible, in public, at the speed
> of light.
>
> **The numbers stations** belong to this period and never really stopped. A
> voice reading digit groups, often for years on a fixed schedule. One-time pads
> are unbreakable when used properly, and a radio broadcast reaches an agent
> without any record of who was listening. The medium's oldest property — that
> receiving leaves no trace — turned out to be an intelligence asset.
>
> **1989 onwards.** The audience the Cold War services existed for could suddenly
> get information other ways. Budgets fell, transmitters were sold or demolished,
> and language services closed by the hundred.
>
> **What is on it now**, and this is the part worth knowing before you assume it
> is dead: broadcasters aimed at places where the internet is filtered, expensive
> or absent; religious broadcasters who bought the surplus transmitters; aircraft
> and ships over oceans where nothing else reaches; time signals; weather fax;
> jammers still running against targets that still broadcast.
>
> **And the reason it keeps mattering.** Every replacement for shortwave requires
> permission from somebody — an ISP, a platform, a government that can throttle a
> connection. Shortwave requires a receiver and an antenna. That is why it is
> still the medium of last resort when a country turns the internet off, and why
> the services that closed keep being partially reopened when somewhere goes
> dark.
